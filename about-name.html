The name

A studio takes something with no nature of its own and decides what it will be. There is an older word for that room.

Bīt šimti

In Akkadian, bīt šimti was the house where the breath of life was breathed in — the chamber where raw material was given a nature and sent out to work. It appears in the Mesopotamian creation accounts as the place humankind was shaped, which is the sense most people meet it in.

The word underneath it is the more useful one. Šimtu does not mean luck, and it does not mean destiny in the sense of something waiting to happen. It means the particular character a thing is assigned at the moment it is made — its nature, fixed at the point of making. A goddess who carried that power was titled banat shimti: maker of fate.

The laboratory

Read that way, the name stops being decoration and becomes a job description. A brief arrives with no character of its own. It leaves with a form, a voice, and something to do in the world. The room where that happens is bīt šimti, and Shimti Multimedia is that room rebuilt with modern instruments — a laboratory rather than a temple, but the same work.

It also settles something that was ambiguous for years. “The Breath of Life in Multimedia” and “Bringing Art To Life” are not two slogans competing for the same idea. They are the same sentence, one said in the older register and one said plainly.

And the other half

The second word is not a category. It is a count. Seventeen disciplines across five practices, held by one person rather than parcelled out to departments — a multi-talent, and so multi in the media.

What that range makes possible is in What we make.
